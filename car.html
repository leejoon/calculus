Your car already knows calculus

A car has two meters. The speedometer shows how fast you’re going right now. The odometer shows how far you’ve gone in total. Calculus is the math that turns one into the other. Press play and watch them together.

The big idea

Speedometer → odometer: add up speed × time. On a graph, that’s the area.

Odometer → speedometer: see how fast the miles go up. On a graph, that’s the slope.

If you know your speed at every moment, you can figure out what the odometer says. If you know the odometer at every moment, you can figure out the speed. The three examples below show how.

Exit 1

From speed to miles

Mr. Lee drives for 4 hours. He goes 30 mph in the city for 1 hour, then 60 mph on the highway for 2 hours, then 20 mph in traffic for 1 hour. The odometer starts at 0. What does it say at the end?

Use the rule distance = speed × time. On a speed graph, speed is the height and time is the width. So each piece of the trip is a rectangle, and its area is the miles.

Answer: 30 + 120 + 20 = 170 miles. A tall rectangle (high speed) adds miles fast. A short one (low speed) adds miles slowly. When speed changes smoothly, we cut the area into many skinny rectangles and add them up. Adding up area under a rate graph is called an integral.

Exit 2

From miles back to speed

Now pretend the speedometer is broken. We only have the odometer. Here is the odometer graph for the same trip. Speed is how many miles the odometer goes up each hour:

speed = change in miles ÷ change in time

On a graph, that’s rise ÷ run, which is the slope.

We get back the same speeds we started with: 30, 60, and 20 mph. A steep line means fast. A flat line means slow. A perfectly flat line means the car is parked.

Exit 3

Speed at one moment

Real cars speed up and slow down smoothly, so the odometer graph is a curve. How do we find the speed at exactly 2:00 pm? Zoom in and use a shorter and shorter time:

Time windowMiles gainedSpeed estimate
2:00 to 2:30 (0.5 hour)27 mi54 mph
2:00 to 2:06 (0.1 hour)5.9 mi59 mph
2:00 to 2:01 (1/60 hour)1 mi60 mph

As the window gets smaller, the answer settles on one number: 60 mph. That’s the speed at that instant. On a graph, it’s the slope of a line that just touches the curve at that point, like the yellow line at the top of this page. Finding this slope is called a derivative.

Check your thinking

Use the trip at the top of the page. Try each one, then open it to check.

At minute 30 the speed is highest. What does the odometer graph look like there?

It’s at its steepest. High speed means the miles go up fastest.

At minutes 0 and 60 the speed is 0. What does the yellow line look like?

It’s flat. The odometer isn’t changing, so the slope is 0.

Can the odometer ever go down? Why or why not?

No. Speed is never negative, so the area under the speed graph only grows.

A car drives 45 mph for 2 hours. What does the odometer add?

45 × 2 = 90 miles. That’s the area of a rectangle 2 wide and 45 tall.

The odometer goes from 200 to 230 miles in half an hour. What was the average speed?

30 ÷ 0.5 = 60 mph. That’s rise ÷ run.

How a computer does it

A car’s computer doesn’t draw graphs. It checks the speed many times each second and keeps a running total. That loop is an integral, one skinny rectangle at a time.

let odometer = 0;          // miles
const timeStep = 1 / 3600; // one second, in hours

function everySecond(speed) {
  odometer = odometer + speed * timeStep;  // add one rectangle
}

Going the other way is a derivative: take two odometer readings, subtract, and divide by the time between them.

function speedFrom(oldMiles, newMiles, hours) {
  return (newMiles - oldMiles) / hours;  // rise ÷ run
}
The speedometer is the slope of the odometer. The odometer is the area under the speedometer.
